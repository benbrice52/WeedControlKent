Scrub Clearance in Kent: When to Cut, What the Rules Say and How to Stop It Coming Back

November 17, 2026 | 7 min read | Written by Weed Control Kent

Scrub builds up quietly. A strip of bramble along an industrial estate boundary, self-seeded sycamore and buddleia in a car park corner, an overgrown embankment nobody has touched for years. Left alone it hides fly-tipping, blocks sight lines and drains, damages fences and hard surfaces, and generates complaints. Clearing it is the easy part. Doing it at the right time of year, within the rules on nesting birds and protected trees, and in a way that stops it growing straight back, is where most jobs go wrong. This guide sets out how commercial scrub clearance should be done in Kent.

What Counts as Scrub Clearance?

Scrub clearance means removing dense, unmanaged woody and thorny growth: bramble, blackthorn and hawthorn thickets, self-seeded saplings such as sycamore, ash and birch, buddleia, elder, and the general tangle that develops on neglected land. It is different from routine grounds maintenance because the plants are established, the volume of material is large and the roots are well developed.

On commercial sites it usually comes up in the same places:

  • Industrial estate and business park boundaries where verges and fence lines have not been managed
  • Car park edges, service yards and loading areas where growth is pushing into hard surfaces
  • Embankments, ditches and retention areas where access is difficult
  • Vacant or part-developed land before landscaping, building work or a sale
  • School and care home boundaries where overgrowth is a security or safety concern

The Best Time of Year to Clear Scrub

In Kent, the best window is September to February. There are three reasons:

  • Birds are not nesting. Wild birds, their nests and eggs are protected by the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981. Most nesting happens between March and August, though it can start earlier and run later for some species.
  • The leaves are down. You can see what is actually there, including fences, drains, uneven ground and anything that has been dumped.
  • The plants are dormant. Cut stump treatment works well through autumn and winter, and ground conditions are generally easier for access.

⚠ Clearing in nesting season is not automatically illegal, but it is risky. It is an offence to intentionally damage or destroy a nest that is in use or being built, or to harm the birds or eggs. If work cannot wait until autumn, the area should be checked by a competent person immediately beforehand and any active nest left undisturbed until the young have fledged.

What to Check Before Any Clearance Starts

A good contractor will check these points at the survey stage, before a price is agreed.

Legal and consent checks

  • Nesting birds (March to August)
  • Tree Preservation Orders on or next to the site
  • Conservation area status
  • Planning conditions on landscaping or retained vegetation
  • Who owns the boundary and the land beyond it

Ground and wildlife checks

  • Reptiles in rough grass and scrub edges
  • Badger setts, bat roosts and, in parts of Kent, hazel dormice
  • Ponds, ditches and watercourses nearby
  • Invasive species hidden in the growth
  • Buried services, fly-tipping and sharps

Trees, conservation areas and protected sites

Ordinary scrub does not need consent, but trees often do. Trees covered by a Tree Preservation Order cannot be cut down or heavily pruned without the council’s permission. In a conservation area, you must give the local authority six weeks’ written notice before cutting most trees with a trunk diameter of 75mm or more, measured 1.5 metres above the ground. Self-seeded saplings that have been left for years can easily pass that size. Mature trees are a job for a qualified tree surgeon, not a scrub clearance crew.

Protected wildlife

Rough scrub is valuable habitat. All native reptiles are protected against being killed or injured, badgers and their setts are protected under the Protection of Badgers Act 1992, bats and their roosts are protected, and hazel dormice, which occur in parts of Kent, are a European protected species. Sites near ponds can also hold great crested newts. If the survey shows signs of any of these, work stops until the right advice has been taken. It is much cheaper to find out before the cutting starts.

Invasive species in the scrub

Dense growth can hide Japanese knotweed, giant hogweed or Himalayan balsam. Cutting or digging through knotweed can spread it, and the waste is controlled. If you see anything suspicious, stop and have it identified first. Our guides to Japanese knotweed removal in Kent and invasive weed removal in Kent explain what to look for.

Why Cutting Alone Does Not Work

The most common mistake with scrub is to cut it down and walk away. Bramble, blackthorn, sycamore, willow and buddleia all regrow vigorously from the roots and stumps, often with several shoots from each stump. A site cut in November can look worse than before by the following September, and you have paid for the same job twice.

The way to deal with this is to treat the stumps with herbicide straight after cutting. The product is applied to the freshly cut surface, before it seals over, and is carried down into the root system. Spot treatment of any regrowth the following growing season picks up what is left. This needs a certified operator: professional herbicide use requires PA1 and PA6 certification, and PA6AW if the work is on or beside water.

A typical scrub clearance programme

  1. Survey (any time of year). Free site visit, checks for the points above and a written quote.
  2. Clearance (September to February). Scrub is cut back to ground level or an agreed height.
  3. Stump treatment (same visit). Cut stumps are treated immediately so the roots take up the herbicide.
  4. Arisings removed. Material is chipped and removed from site, or taken away by a registered waste carrier.
  5. Follow-up (the following growing season). Regrowth is spot-treated before it re-establishes.
  6. Ongoing control. A seasonal programme stops the area reverting. See our commercial weed control contracts guide.

What Happens to the Waste?

Scrub clearance creates a lot of material. On a commercial site, it is business waste, which means the landowner has a duty of care to make sure it is handled legally, whoever does the cutting. Check that the contractor chips and removes it themselves or uses a registered waste carrier, and ask for the paperwork. Burning waste from a business site is restricted, can cause smoke complaints, and is rarely the easiest route. Make sure the quote states what is happening to the arisings, because it is one of the main differences between a cheap quote and a complete one.

What Affects the Cost of Scrub Clearance?

We do not quote from photos, because the same patch of bramble can be an hour’s work or several days. The things that move the price are:

  • The area, and how dense and tall the growth is
  • The type of plant. Mature blackthorn and sycamore are slower than bramble
  • Access, slopes and how close the work is to fences, buildings, water or roads
  • How much material there is and where it can go
  • Whether stump treatment and follow-up visits are included
  • Any survey or consent work needed first

Frequently Asked Questions

When is the best time of year to clear scrub in Kent?

Between September and February. Birds are not nesting, the leaves are down so the ground is easier to assess, and the plants are going dormant. Clearing between March and August is not banned, but nesting birds are protected under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, so the area has to be checked first and any active nest left alone.

Is it enough to just cut the scrub back?

Usually not. Bramble, blackthorn, sycamore, willow and buddleia all regrow from the roots or stumps, and cut growth often returns within a season. Treating the cut stumps with herbicide straight after cutting, and spot-treating any regrowth the following year, is what stops it coming back.

Do I need permission to clear scrub or small trees?

Not for ordinary scrub, but you do for trees protected by a Tree Preservation Order, and in a conservation area you must give the council six weeks' written notice before cutting most trees. Self-seeded saplings can fall into this category once the trunk reaches 75mm across, so check before work starts.

What happens to the cuttings?

Waste from a commercial site is business waste, so it has to be dealt with legally. In practice the material is chipped and taken away or reused with the landowner's agreement, or removed by a registered waste carrier. Burning waste on a business site is restricted and is rarely the simplest option.

Can you clear scrub next to a ditch, pond or stream?

Yes, but it needs extra care. Herbicide used on or near water has to be applied by an operator with the aquatic certification (PA6AW), and bank vegetation often shelters protected wildlife. We hold PA6AW and will say in the survey if any extra consent is needed.
